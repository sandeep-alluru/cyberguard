## 1. The problem

### 1.1 What is broken in the market as AI evolves

The external research converges on a market that is not short of capability and is short of **attention**.

**The attack side got faster, and the defender side did not.** Average eCrime breakout time fell to **29 minutes in 2025, 65 % faster than 2024**, with the fastest observed intrusion at 27 seconds and exfiltration beginning within 4 minutes in one case [R1]. Mandiant's M-Trends 2026 independently found median access-to-handoff collapsed from **8+ hours (2022) to 22 seconds (2025)** [R1]. Median dwell time, meanwhile, **rose to 14 days** in 2025 — 25 days when an outsider was the one who told you — and only **52 % of breaches were detected by the organisation itself** [R1]. Attackers now log in with stolen credentials and live inside legitimate tools: **82 % of detections were malware-free** [R1].

**The defender side is buried in a queue it cannot work.** An average SOC receives **960 alerts/day**, large enterprises **3,181/day across ~28 tools**, **~40 % of alerts are never investigated**, and median time to first triage is **56 minutes** — against a 29-minute attacker breakout time, so **the average alert is first touched after the average attacker has already moved laterally** [R3, R6]. Independent surveys agree: 73 % of teams name false positives as their top detection challenge [R3]; 43 % of teams still have >5 % of high-priority alerts unresolved a week later [R3]. Mid-market specifics are worse: a median team spends **20 minutes dismissing a single junk alert**, and for 25 % of organisations manual triage consumes **214 hours/week** [R6].

**AI has made the attacker's edge concentrated in exactly two artefacts: language, and triage.** Microsoft's Digital Defense Report 2025 (vendor telemetry) reports AI-generated phishing at a **54 % click-through rate against 12 %** for manually written phishing — recipients 4.5× more likely to click — and a Harvard-affiliated controlled study found fully automated AI spear-phishing matching human expert performance at roughly **$0.04 per email** in API cost against ~30 minutes of human time per message [R2]. Anthropic's own scanning program (per industry reporting) surfaced **~29,000 candidate vulnerabilities** in open-source projects with only **~516 patched upstream** — discovery is outrunning the ecosystem's ability to absorb it, which is a triage problem [R2].

**And AI has become a new target class with named CVEs.** EchoLeak (CVE-2025-32711) defeated Microsoft 365 Copilot's own prompt-injection classifier with a single never-opened email; CurXecute (CVE-2025-54135) turned a repo file into remote code execution on a developer machine [R2, R4]. Microsoft's AI security layer was the thing being bypassed [R4].

**The demand side has a calendar and a channel.** ISC2's 2025 Workforce Study (16,029 professionals) found **95 % report at least one skills gap**, **59 % call gaps critical or significant** (up from 44 %), and **88 % have already suffered a significant security consequence caused by a skills shortage**; the #1 most-needed skill is **AI (41 %)** [R3, R6]. MSSPs already pay **~$9–10 per AI investigation** (Prophet Security ~$50 K/yr for 5,000 investigations) [R6] — so willingness to pay for a *worked investigation* is priced and observable. And an estimated **14–19 % of enterprise LLM deployments were air-gapped or fully on-prem by Q1 2026**, up from ~7 % a year earlier, concentrated in defense, healthcare and regulated finance [R6].

**The synthesis of the external evidence is this:** the market's problem is not "we lack a smart chatbot". It is **unworked alert queues and scarce analyst hours** [R6], and the tooling that is bought is bought to **retire analyst minutes**, not to demonstrate autonomy [R3].

### 1.2 Where the external rounds and the project record disagree — stated plainly

Disagreements between the external research and the programme's own record are stated here rather than smoothed over. There are three real disagreements, and one of them changes the recommendation.

**Disagreement 1 — the record's corpus supports a different product than the market's largest pain.**
Round 3 and Round 6 put alert triage and false-positive suppression at the centre of buyer demand. The project's own corpus, measured today, contains **452 rows (0.32 %) of alert-triage workflow, 11 rows (0.008 %) of false-positive analysis, 3 rows (0.002 %) of IOC extraction and exactly 1 row (0.0007 %) of log/event summarisation** across all 140,545 rows [PR-R1]. The shapes the market most wants are the shapes the corpus most lacks. This is not a close call and it is not fixable by prompt engineering.

**Disagreement 2 — the external research assumes a clean corpus; the corpus is not clean.**
Round 7's shape (e1) says CVE/CWE/CVSS records are "public, structured, and license-clean — the *easiest corpus to qualify*, sidestepping CyberGuard's current data-quality debt" [R7]. That is true of the *replacement* corpus and false of the *existing* one: of the two streams that teach this capability, **primus is 100 % contaminated** with `<|reserved_special_token_N|>` and **merged concatenates reasoning with answers** [ER §3.3, PR-R1]. The shape is right; the data has to be rebuilt, not repaired.

**Disagreement 3 — the local-deployment moat is weaker for the recommended shape than for the money shapes.**
Round 6's central argument for a local model is **data custody**: "an MSSP holding 50 clients' logs cannot route them through a shared external API without 50 contractual conversations" [R6, R7 shape (d)]. **CVE descriptions are public data.** The custody argument therefore does *not* apply to the shape this document recommends; it applies to the shapes the corpus cannot support. Round 5's own rationale for CyberSecQwen-4B cites exactly this — frontier models "ship prompts to someone else's datacenter" and are trained to refuse messy defensive edge cases, which matters in "air-gapped/partially-connected environments" [R5]. For a public CVE feed, the local win is **zero marginal cost and offline operation**, not custody.

**INFERENCE.** This disagreement is the reason the recommendation below is honest rather than tidy: **the shape with the best public-proof story is not the shape with the strongest commercial moat, and the shape with the strongest commercial moat is not provable on public benchmarks.** Round 7 reached the same conclusion from the outside — "the one shape the evidence argues **against** leading with is (d): no public proof exists for it, and unprovable claims are what killed CyberGuard v3's credibility the first time" [R7].

### 1.3 The problem this product solves

**INFERENCE, from [R1]–[R7] and [PR-R1]–[PR-R5] together.** A security team must decide, every week, which of a stream of newly published vulnerabilities actually matters to it — and that decision is made by reading CVE text, by hand, with a tool that helps little. The work is **classification and prioritisation over a public feed that never stops**. It is unglamorous, recurring, batch-shaped, and currently done by hand.

This is the same primitive the market's biggest pain is made of — *classify, prioritise, decide* — applied to the part of the input stream that is public, structured, license-clean, and therefore the part that can be solved **without collecting anybody's private data**. That is why it is the right first product here and not the biggest one.

## 2. The product — one recommendation

### 2.1 The product, named plainly

> **CyberGuard VulnTriage** — a locally-run 4B model that turns a CVE or advisory into a **CWE**, a **CVSS v3.1 vector and score**, a **plain-English impact statement**, and a **prioritisation call**, and that **does not refuse legitimate defensive analysis of a vulnerability**.
>
> **The open release:** `CyberGuard-Vuln-4B` — `Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507` + a tuned adapter, Apache-2.0, with weights, training recipe, dataset card, a public evaluation harness, and a benchmark table containing its own bad news.

**The user.** A vulnerability-management or AppSec-adjacent team — typically 1–5 security staff, or an IT generalist wearing the security hat — who must decide which of this week's CVEs matter to them [R6, R7 shape (e1)]. Also the individual practitioner: a consultant, a student, an engineer doing defensive work on a laptop with no network [R5].

**The job.** Given a CVE description or advisory, output, in a fixed schema: **CWE** · **CVSS v3.1 vector and score** · **affected-component plain-English summary** · **exploitability note** · **prioritisation call** (patch now / schedule / monitor). Run over a whole feed, not one at a time.

**The name of the thing being optimised.** Round 3's synthesis of practitioner behaviour is the most useful sentence in the external research: *"the binding problem was trust calibration, not capability"* [R3]. So the product's contract is not "accurate" — it is **calibrated**, and it has **two** halves, both of which the current artifact fails in a specific, measurable way:

1. **It answers when it can, says so when it cannot, and never silently refuses legitimate defensive work.** v3 fails this at 30.67 % against the base model's 4.27 % on MITRE- (re-measured here: 29.07 % vs 4.13 %, reproducing the record at p = 0.111) on MITRE-FRR. **The number that matters for the competitive claim is smaller but harder: the public comparator's own false-refusal rate is 1.07 % (re-measured exactly), and the BEST arm this programme ever produced — v1 — is still 9.87 pp worse than that (10.93 %, p = 1.4 × 10⁻¹⁹). No tuned arm reaches the public model's rate [ER §5.3.2].**
2. **It answers in the shape the caller asked for.** v3 fails this too: 40.88 % of its CTI-MCQ answers and 18.70 % of its CTI-RCM answers fail the last-line parse — and *because* of that failure it loses, on the instrument's headline, to a comparator it actually **beats on content** by +10.70 pp and +3.69 pp [ER §5.3.7].

That is the product thesis: **calibrated and schema-faithful.** A vulnerability-analysis tool that refuses a legitimate CVE analysis, or answers with prose when the caller asked for a CWE id, is not an analyst's tool — and both of those failures are measurable, fixable, and (for the second) already fixable without touching the weights.

### 2.2 Why a small, locally-run, tuned model is the right form — and where it is not

**INFERENCE, from [R5], [R6], [R7] and [PR-R4].**

**Why it fits this job:**

1. **The job is batch classification, which is the canonical small-model workload.** CTI-RCM is a public, free-form CVE→CWE task; CTI-VSP is CVSS prediction. A 4B model running on one GPU at batch is the right form factor, and Gartner's prediction that enterprises will use small task-specific models ~3× more than general-purpose LLMs by 2027 rests on exactly this class [R6].
2. **The feed is continuous and the marginal cost must be zero.** The CVE feed never stops [R7 shape (e1)]. Cloud API cost scales with volume; a local model does not. Self-hosted small-model running costs for ~10,000 queries/day are commonly estimated at $500–2,000/month against $5,000–50,000 for equivalent frontier-API usage [R6 — blog-tier estimate, directional].
3. **Offline and air-gapped operation is a real eligibility criterion for part of the market** (14–19 % of enterprise deployments air-gapped or fully on-prem [R6]) — weaker here than for private logs, but real for defense, healthcare and regulated finance, and free once the model is local.
4. **A fine-tune binds a schema; a general API does not.** Triage and prioritisation output must land in the same shape every time, and "a fine-tune binds format in a way prompt engineering on a general API model does not guarantee across vendor model updates" [R7 shape (a)]. This project has the receipt for that claim in both directions: v3's whole-response parse rate on CyberMetric went from **55 % (base) to 88.6 %** purely from format training [PR-R1, ER §5.3] — and yet its *last-line* parse rate on CTI-MCQ is **40.88 % unparseable**, so the format training did not transfer to every output contract [ER §5.3.7]. The lesson for the product is that the schema must be enforced by the serving layer as well as by the model.
5. **The direct twin proves the form is legible to the field.** CyberSecQwen-4B is the same base family, the same size class, the same defensive positioning, Apache-2.0, and it published its full recipe (LoRA r=64, 10 epochs, bf16) [R5]. The genre works; the scoreboard is public and stable [R5].

**Where it is not right, stated honestly:**

- **It is not right for novel exploit analysis or root-cause discovery.** That needs frontier reasoning. Round 4's pattern is unambiguous: every success case pairs a model with an **independent verification step** — Project Zero reproduced Big Sleep's finding before patching; XBOW's moat is its automated validator that kills false positives [R4]. **A small model alone is not the product; it is the proposal step in a proposal → validate → human-gate loop.** The report must not claim otherwise.
- **It is not right for the decision.** "Is this finding real?" — the MSSP's hardest question — is a judgement the model should abstain from, and the corpus has **11 rows** of false-positive analysis to teach it [PR-R1].
- **It is not the shape with the strongest commercial moat.** See §1.2, disagreement 3: custody is the local advantage, and CVE text is public.

### 2.3 Why this shape and not another — the short version

| if the priority is | then the shape is | but |
|---|---|---|
| **authority / proof (the stated primary goal)** | **(e1) vulnerability triage — RECOMMENDED** | it wins on proof and build cost, not on revenue moat |
| revenue / a business | (a) alert triage or (d) MSSP summariser | 8–12 weeks of data collection, unprovable on public benchmarks [PR-R4, R7] |
| a benchmark redemption story | (c) classifier/guard | the guard shelf is saturated at every size, including Qwen3Guard 4B, Apache-2.0, free [R5] |

**INFERENCE.** Because the primary goal is authority and a demonstration of tuning capability [R7 shape (e2), R8], and because the standing rule is public-benchmarks-only, the product must be the shape that can be **fully proven on public benchmarks by a stranger**. That is (e1) or (b). Between them, (e1) is better because: its corpus is **public, structured and license-clean** and can be qualified from scratch rather than repaired [R7]; its job is **batch** rather than conversational, which suits a 4B; it has a **complete public scoreboard** (CTI-RCM, CTI-VSP, CTI-MCQ); and it has a **recurring feed** as its driver. §4 works through every alternative properly.

## 3. Why this problem is worth solving — market and user evidence, not enthusiasm

**The job exists and is paid for.** Vulnerability management is one of the three use cases IBM reports breached organisations most intend to hand to AI systems — alert triage, **vulnerability management**, and scanning/pentesting — with three-quarters of breached organisations planning higher AI deployment in those areas [R3]. **The edge is where the damage is:** Verizon DBIR 2025 reports edge-device and VPN vulnerabilities grew **eightfold** as an attack focus, only **54 % were fully patched**, median remediation time **32 days**, while exploitation grew 34 % YoY [R1]. Patch prioritisation is therefore not a convenience; it is the gap where a 32-day remediation time meets a 29-minute breakout time.

**The people who would do this work cannot be hired at the rate needed.** ISC2: 95 % report a skills gap, 59 % call it critical or significant, 88 % have already suffered a significant security consequence from it, 33 % lack resources to staff adequately [R3, R6]. The #1 most-needed skill is AI at 41 % [R3].

**The buyer pays for the outcome, and the outcome is countable.** AI SOC products price per investigation at ~$9–10 [R6]; managed SMB security runs $500–2,500/month for 1–50 staff and $2,500–12,000/month for mid-market, against $90–120 K/year for one mid-tier US analyst [R3]. "Any product shape that doesn't visibly retire analyst minutes has no budget line" [R6].

**The audience for the authority goal is the same audience.** Round 6's closing point: MSSP practitioners, SOC leads and the open-model community "are exactly the people who read CTI-Bench tables and hire for demonstrated model skill" [R6]. Round 8's finding is that hiring managers screen **evaluation discipline first** — "has the candidate built a reference set, scored model outputs against it, and used the scores to make a release decision?" [R8]. The audience for the product and the audience for the authority are the same people.

**The scope of this evidence.** No source found directly surveys "SOCs willing to pay for a local security model" as a category — because the category barely exists commercially [R6]. The evidence for willingness to pay is **structural**, not surveyed: per-investigation pricing proves teams pay ~$9–10 for an AI-worked alert; the air-gapped share proves a significant minority cannot buy the cloud version at any price; MSSP margin math proves flat-cost local inference is worth money to the channel [R6]. **A small local model sits at the intersection of those three proofs — that is the strongest honest claim the research supports** [R6].

## 5. What success looks like — public benchmarks only, bars named in advance

### 5.1 The bar sheet, fixed before the next training run

All instruments are public third-party suites. All protocol parameters are stated so a stranger can regenerate the numbers, which is what post-"Leaderboard Illusion" practice requires [R9].

**One measured fact about this bar sheet that changes how it should be read, and it was measured on 2026-10-23.** The sheet contains **two free-form instruments (B1 CTI-RCM, B5 CTI-MCQ)** and **two multiple-choice ones (B6 CyberMetric, B7 SecQA)**, and the two kinds measure different things. The multiple-choice benchmarks score the **entire** response against a one-letter contract, so they measure **format compliance as much as knowledge** — the untrained base model answers them as *"B. IP"*, scores **0.4720** on CyberMetric for that reason alone, and **0.8840** when the answer is read instead of the formatting; **91.2 %** of its format-failure rows carry the correct answer [ER §5.3.7b]. **The free-form instruments are where the content claim lives**, which is why **B1 and B5 carry the headline and B6 and B7 carry the unparseable rate**, and why this sheet now says explicitly *not* to publish a both-parseable number as a content figure — that method is selection-biased exactly where the arms' format compliance differs, and it was corrected in [ER §5.3.7b]. **A release table that prints the strict number and the unparseable rate side by side is honest under either reading; a table that prints only the strict number is not.**

**Protocol to be written into the model card's notes field** (round 10's rule: *"Nobody verifies rows centrally, so this is your credibility"* [R10]): benchmark versions and splits; the base model in the same table under the same protocol; named peer models in the same size class; sample counts; greedy decode, 1 trial, 512 new tokens, batch 8, left padding with explicit attention mask, BF16, no quantisation, PEFT unmerged, own tokenizer/template, one user message, no system message, zero demonstrations, no truncation; the harness commit; and the command to regenerate.

**Three rules for this bar sheet, taken directly from the external research:**

- **A table containing its own bad news is the one practitioners forward to each other** [R10]. The FRR column stays in the table even if the number is bad.
- **A score on a self-built benchmark is not merely weak evidence — post-2025 it is read as a negative signal** [R9]. Hence: public suites only, and no in-house scorecard in the release.
- **Report the headline AND the both-parseable number, with the unparseable rate between them** [ER §5.3.7]. The frozen headline is the instrument's number and is what the bar is judged on; but a reader who sees only the headline will draw the wrong conclusion about the model, and this project has already demonstrated that in both directions. The pair tells the truth and costs one extra column.

**A fourth requirement, which is a product requirement rather than a reporting one.** Because v3's CTI deficits are an **output-contract failure** and not a knowledge failure (§5.3.7), the product must ship with an **output contract** as part of its interface: the model answers in the schema the caller asked for, and the caller can rely on it. A product whose differentiating claim is "it reasons better than the public comparator" cannot simultaneously ship a 40.88 % parse failure on its own headline metric. Two candidate mechanisms, neither requiring a retrain: a **constrained last-line instruction** in the serving template, or a **parse-and-retry** wrapper that asks the model to emit only the token when the first answer does not parse. Both must be measured on the frozen surface before either is claimed.

**And a fifth, which this evaluation's truncation measurement makes necessary.** A parse-and-retry can only recover a response that *reached* an answer. On CTI-MCQ, the population-level decode-cap truncation rate is **UNMEASURED** — the stored field is the batch maximum, not the item's own count, and the frozen runner records it as INVALID for interpretation (`docs/ESCALATION-E2-T-TRUNCATION-FIELD-WITHDRAWAL.md`) — and **only 9.88 %** of the rejected-and-unparseable ones had already stated the correct answer — **4.04 pp** [ER §5.3.14]. So on the free-form reasoning benchmarks the output contract needs a **length discipline** as well as a format constraint — a bounded reasoning budget, a terse-answer instruction, or a decode budget large enough to finish — and the CTI-MCQ bar (B5) has to report the truncation rate beside the parse rate so that a reader can tell which of the two failures they are looking at. **On CTI-RCM this is not a live issue** (40.1 % of the rejected rows recover as correct), which is why B1's output-contract route stands and B5's does not.

### 5.2 What the bar sheet deliberately does *not* include

**INFERENCE.** The following must not appear in the release as proof, because no public instrument measures them and the standing rule bars inventing one [R7]: triage quality, false-positive rate on real traffic, log-summary quality, rule correctness, and semantic usefulness of a non-refusal. Each is either covered by a named public suite elsewhere (harmful compliance → HarmBench; false-refusal → FRR) or is **demonstrated in public examples** rather than scored [R7].

### 5.3 The release decision rule, stated in advance

**Ship** if B1–B4 hold and B5–B7 are published with their parse rates and baselines.
**Do not ship** if B3 fails — a model that refuses a legitimate vulnerability analysis is not a security analyst's tool, and this project's own record shows that failing on a safety-usability bar is the one outcome the release rule exists to prevent.
**If B1 fails but B3 passes**, the honest release is the *evidence stack* (§6) plus a smaller claim, not a benchmark claim.

## 6. The authority design

### 6.1 The thesis, from the external rounds

**For authority purposes, CyberGuard's product is the public evidence stack — a qualified security corpus, a fine-tune measured only on public benchmarks, and an honest release record — with the model as its centrepiece and the publishing operation as its distribution** [R10]. "The model gets him considered; the stack gets him believed."

**The most important finding of the external research for this project is that the failure record is the strongest asset, not the weakest.** Round 9 argues the point from the field's own canonical example: Berkeley's *"The False Promise of Imitating Proprietary LLMs"* became one of the most-cited evaluation papers of the open-LLM era and a career asset for its authors **because it was a rigorous negative result the hype cycle needed** [R9]. Round 9 then applies it directly: *"Published straight, that is a 'False Promise'-class artifact: it demonstrates the author can (a) detect a broken first-party metric, (b) re-measure on public instruments, and (c) report a result that argues against his own model. For the hiring-manager audience … that write-up may be the single most valuable page CyberGuard produces, worth more than the adapter itself"* [R9]. Round 10 agrees: a dataset card documenting the qualified rebuild "converts the program's worst fact into its most senior-looking artifact" [R10].

**INFERENCE.** CyberGuard's actual position is unusual and worth stating: **the evaluation phase that just completed is already the strongest artefact in the portfolio.** A retired first-party instrument produced a wrong release verdict by 17 points; the figure was corrected to −0.727 pp; the public re-measurement reproduced the recorded numbers to within 0.91 pp; a false-refusal regression of +26.4 pp was found and confirmed; and — this week — a third of the programme's own defect counts were shown not to survive re-measurement, and were withdrawn in writing. Round 8's hiring screen asks exactly one question first: *"Has the candidate built a reference set, scored model outputs against it, and used the scores to make a release decision?"* [R8]. ### 7.2 Five cheap fixes that may remove both headline problems without training

**INFERENCE, and it is worth attention because all five are nearly free.**

**Fix 1 — the evaluation-surface hypothesis: TESTED, AND REFUTED.** This was the cheapest possible fix, so it was worth testing before anything else: if v3's false-refusal rate is a property of the *evaluation surface* rather than the model, then serving it with the system prompt it was trained on would fix it at zero cost, in one line of configuration [ER §7 P0-7]. **It was measured, and the answer is the opposite.** All three training personas, verbatim from `train_cyberguard.py`, were run on the same 250 prompts under the same instrument: every one of them **raises** v3's false-refusal rate — **+7.20 pp** (p = 0.0153), **+6.80 pp** (p = 0.0115), **+11.20 pp** (p = 0.000307), exact McNemar on the discordant pairs — against a no-system rate of **36.80 %** and a base-model rate of **3.20 %** on the same rows [ER §5.3.4]. **Serving the model with the system prompt it was trained on would make the product worse.** This option is now closed, and it was worth closing: it is the fix a team reaches for first, because it is free.

**Fix 2 — the refusal rate's cause is now PROVEN, and the lever is a mixture reweight.** This is the single most important finding for the product. The v2 arms are the same 81,074-row mixtures differing in one swapped stream — the refusal slot — and this phase ran them on the same 750 benign prompts with the same instrument: **arm A refuses 35.33 %, arm B refuses 17.47 %**, a **17.87 pp** difference at **exact McNemar p = 5.1 × 10⁻²²** [ER §5.3.9]. v3 carries arm A's stream in full (1,761 of arm A's 1,779 scorer-flagged rows (98.99 %), by prompt signature) and its rate is 30.67 % — in arm A's band. **So the false-refusal regression is a property of the training mixture, not a model pathology**, and the programme had already built the experiment that proves it without ever running it. The fix is to reweight or remove the refusal stream, and **arm B is the existing evidence that this moves the number** — it needs no new training to test the direction, only a decision about the target weight. **The claim's own limit, measured and stated rather than left implicit:** the pair identifies the refusal slot's *content* as causal, not the refusal behaviour inside it — within the 5,600 substituted rows the content type, the prompt diversity (700 distinct prompts against the control's 228) and the answer length (a 15.5 % gap) all move together, and the programme's own SPEC-V2 D2 gate for a matched control reads NOT MET [ER §5.3.6, §5.3.9]. **The lever and the fix are unaffected; the mechanism is claimed at the resolution the evidence supports.**

**And the evaluation has now measured the dose-response, which quantifies the lever and bounds the prize.** Counting the refusal rows directly in each arm's own mixture file gives a monotonic curve: **4.91 % refusal share → 17.47 % FRR** (arm B), **6.82 % → 29.07 %** (v3), **11.82 % → 35.33 %** (arm A) [ER §5.3.9b]. Two things fall out of it. First, **v2 arm A and v3 carry the same 1,198 distinct refusal rows** (content-identical) — the difference between their doses is pure **dilution** (11.82 % of an 81,074-row mixture against 6.82 % of a 140,545-row one), and dilution alone is worth **−6.27 pp** of FRR. A free lever the programme already pulled without noticing. Second, and less comfortably: **the untrained base model already refuses 4.13 % with no refusal training at all**, so about **3.07 pp of the false-refusal rate is the base model's own**, and **no reweighting of this corpus can reach the public comparator's 1.07 %**. A corpus-only remedy bottoms out at roughly the base model's own **4.13 %** — and even that floor is an inference rather than a measured point, because the base model carries no adapter. **The competitive claim therefore needs either a different base model or a different claim** — which is a product decision, not an engineering one, and it should be taken before the next training run is commissioned rather than after.

**And the evaluation has now measured what that lever costs on the other end, so the fix can no longer be sold as one-sided.** The HarmBench re-judgement puts all six arms on the **harmful side** of the same axis, and across the four trained arms the false-refusal rate and the harmful-compliance rate correlate at **−0.87** [ER §5.3.3]: **the refusal stream is one dial, and turning it down to fix false refusals turns up harmful compliance.** The specific price is now measurable rather than hypothetical — **v1 buys the family's best false-refusal rate (10.93 %) at 12/159 harmful compliance, worse than the untrained base model's 1/159** (paired, p = 9.8 × 10⁻⁴), while **v3 buys 0/159 at a 29.07 % false-refusal rate**. Arm B, which is the concrete evidence that the lever moves, lands at **17.47 % FRR and 5/159** — its harmful-side movement versus arm A is **+1.89 pp and not statistically significant** (p = 0.375). **So the honest form of Fix 2 is: it is a reweighting of a two-sided dial, not a one-way repair, and the target weight has to be chosen against both instruments at once.** A product spec that optimises MITRE-FRR alone will produce a model that refuses a third of benign security requests; one that optimises HarmBench alone will produce v3. **No tuned arm clears both ends today — only the untrained base model does**, which is the strongest argument in this document for rebuilding the corpus rather than reweighting it.

**Fix 3 — the CTI deficits are an output-contract artefact, but the contract alone will not recover all of it, and the evaluation now measures exactly why.** On the rows where v3 and the public comparator both produce a parseable answer, v3 is **+10.70 pp ahead on CTI-MCQ and +3.69 pp ahead on CTI-RCM** — yet its headlines are 10.96 pp and 4.60 pp *behind*, because 40.88 % and 18.70 % of its answers fail the last-line parse [ER §5.3.7]. A permissive extractor applied to the *stored* responses recovers **+6.40 pp on CTI-MCQ (0.4680 → 0.5320)** and **+7.80 pp on CTI-RCM (0.6330 → 0.7110)** — and on CTI-RCM that is **enough to beat the comparator's 0.6790** [ER §5.3.7, `output-contract-recovery-ceiling.json`]. The fix is a serving-template constraint or a parse-and-retry wrapper, measured on the frozen surface before it is claimed.

**But this evaluation has now measured the mechanism underneath, on the full stored population of 12,330 responses across three models and four public benchmarks, and it splits the two benchmarks apart.** The population-level decode-cap truncation rate is **UNMEASURED** — the stored `generated_tokens`/`termination` field is the batch maximum rather than the item's own count (100 % of the 8-item batches carry one identical value, for every arm), and the frozen runner records it as INVALID for interpretation; the label supports only the bound (**v3/CTI-MCQ ∈ [12.12 %, 96.80 %]**, **v3/CTI-RCM ∈ [10.10 %, 80.00 %]**) [`docs/ESCALATION-E2-T-TRUNCATION-FIELD-WITHDRAWAL.md`]. **Every one of v3's CTI parse failures sits in a batch that contained at least one cap hit** — 1,022 of 1,022 on CTI-MCQ and 187 of 187 on CTI-RCM — and **none of the 80 CTI-MCQ responses that finished inside the cap failed to parse** (P(unparseable | finished) = 0.0000), so v3 has no parse failure outside a batch that contained a cap hit [ER §5.3.14]. The comparator fails the *other* way: 67 of its 71 CTI-MCQ parse failures are format failures, not truncations. Reading the model's own statements out of the rejected responses, **only 9.88 % had already committed to the correct answer** (101 of the 1,022 unparseable responses, **4.04 pp** of strict accuracy); the other 833 were **still reasoning and had reached no answer** — a response-text fact, measured on the unparseable set, and independent of the withdrawn field. So:

- **CTI-RCM keeps the cheap fix.** 19.70 % unparseable, and **79 of 197** rejected rows recover as correct (**40.1 %**) — a parse-and-retry or schema constraint genuinely pays here, and it is enough to put v3 ahead of the comparator.
- **CTI-MCQ does not.** A last-line parse-and-retry cannot recover a response that never stated an answer. There the remedy is **corpus-side first** (Fix 4: strip the contaminated rows, which shortens the template) and, second, **a decode budget large enough to finish** — which is a change to the decision surface, so it needs a decision rather than a configuration tweak [ER §8 question 9]. The prize is bounded before it is delivered: **about 4 pp** on CTI-MCQ (101 responses), not the 11 pp the headline gap suggests.

**Fix 4 — the CTI deficits are a CORPUS defect, traced end to end.** This is the strongest finding in the evaluation and it changes where the work goes. **v3 emits the literal string `<|reserved_special_token_0|>` at the start of 100 % of its CTI-MCQ responses (2500/2500) and 100 % of its CTI-RCM responses (1000/1000), and 0 % of its CyberMetric and SecQA responses.** The base model and the comparator emit it 0 % everywhere [ER §5.3.8]. Those 4,891 rows are the contaminated Primus stream (failure A1) — 3.48 % of the training corpus — and they teach not a prefix but a *reasoning-then-answer template*. v3 reverts to that template on exactly the two benchmarks whose prompts share the contaminated rows' task family, and its answer then fails the frozen last-line parser. **So the "v3 is weak at security knowledge" verdict was the record measuring its own corpus defect at inference time.** Stripping 4,891 rows from the corpus is the cheapest possible way to recover the CTI headroom — and the corpus rebuild in step 7 has to happen anyway.

**Fix 5 — the corpus also teaches an output habit the release contract forbids, and the fix is already decided.** Reading the training targets directly — the same file, sha256-verified against the training receipt's `arms_sha256` — shows that **18,074 rows (12.86 % of the corpus; 78.087 % of the `merged` stream) teach the model to narrate the request instead of answering it**: the target's first line reads *"First, the user is asking about CWE-319: Cleartext Transmission of Sensitive Information…"*. The project's own release contract requires *"Zero stock preambles or thinking-control tokens across the release battery"* [ER §3.3c; the programme's plan, line 181] and the pilot spec gates them at *"≤ 1 % of knowledge responses"*. **A model trained on 12.86 % preamble-opening targets is being trained into a gate it will be measured against**, and every shape in this document has a human reading its output.

The part that makes this cheap: **the exclusion of "Primus and the merged reasoning corpus" was already decided on 2026-10-04** — **28,037 rows, 19.95 % of the mixture** — and the failure register's own verdict for A1 and A7 is *"PRESCRIBED (not verified)"*. v3 trained 2026-09-30 → 2026-10-01, so that exclusion postdates it: this is the rule the *next* corpus must satisfy, not a rule v3 broke. The one removal with no outstanding authority is the one that fixes the most [ER §7 P1-10].

**AND IT IS NOW EXECUTED (2026-10-23).** The removal set is a written, counted artefact (`data/v4/removal-set-d5.jsonl`, 28,037 row_ids, every row's line hash recomputed from the mixture and every source attribution confirmed against the v4 programme's independently tagged rows — 28,037 of 28,037, 0 disagreements), and the rebuilt corpus carries **0 leading thinking-control tokens** and **0 stock-preamble targets** where the source had 4,891 and 18,074. **A finding the programme did not have when this document was written:** the removal of the two excluded sources *raised* the refusal share from 6.82 % to 8.52 %, because it shrank the corpus while the refusal stream stayed at 9,584 presentations — so the reweight that Fix 2 calls for had to be solved against the **final** denominator, not the old one. The refusal share is now **4.91 %** — the lowest share for which the programme has a *measured* false-refusal rate and a *measured* harmful-compliance count — executed as decision **W-3** and open to change [`docs/REFUSAL-POLICY.md`].

**Fix 6 — the product's output contract now exists, is machine-checkable, and the refusal posture is decided.** §3.2 of this document argued that the product must ship with an *output contract*, and that "the model answers in the shape the caller asked for" is one of the two halves of the product thesis. That contract is now written: **`docs/PRODUCT-CONTRACT.md`** defines **`TRIAGE-1`**, a **six-key** schema — `cwe`, `cvss_vector`, `severity`, `priority`, `rationale`, `abstain` — with both enums exhaustive (severity NONE/LOW/MEDIUM/HIGH/CRITICAL/UNKNOWN, priority patch_now/schedule/monitor/escalate), every field typed and required, and three consistency rules machine-checked. **The schema is machine-checkable in the strict sense:** the checker *parses the schema out of the contract document* rather than restating it, so the contract and the corpus cannot drift apart. Three decisions inside it are the product's own, and each is recorded with its reason: (1) **the CVSS score is delivered by the deterministic validator, not by the model**, because a model-emitted score could only disagree with the vector it was handed; (2) **the abstain path is a first-class output** with its own measured rate, kept separate from the false-refusal rate because the two failure modes are opposites; and (3) **the refusal posture is refuse-and-surface** — the job is batch triage over a feed, so refuse-and-stop would *silently drop items* the user cannot distinguish from harmless ones, and a refusal over a real CVE still carries the triage the analyst is entitled to. The corpus now carries the matching supervision: **352** triage-format rows and **144** triage-shaped harmful requests, the latter across four framings including a claimed authorisation and a classroom framing — because a cover that does not change the purpose does not buy an answer.

**INFERENCE — and one warning to read before spending anything.** Taken together, these five findings change the shape of the programme. The two headline numbers that make v3 look weak — a 24.93-point false-refusal regression (29.07 % vs base 4.13 % re-measured here; the record's 30.67 % vs 4.27 % reproduces at p = 0.111) and an 11-point CTI-MCQ deficit — are **both corpus- or serving-side**, not model-side, and **both have a known, cheap, testable fix**. The first act of this product is **configuration, measurement and corpus hygiene**; the retrain may not be needed for the headline bars at all. It also makes the release's story stronger, not weaker: "here is the model, here is the surface it was measured on, here is the contamination we found in our own data, what it cost us, and the experiment that proves it" is exactly the kind of finding the hiring-manager audience in [R8] and the practitioner audience in [R9] reward.

**INFERENCE.** This also makes the sequencing right: **fix the serving surface, reweight the refusal stream and strip the contaminated rows before spending GPU on the corpus rebuild.** If the FRR problem is largely a surface artefact and the CTI deficit is an output contract plus a known corpus defect, then step 9 (training) may be unnecessary for the headline bars, and the whole programme collapses from "37 GPU-hours" to "weeks of operator-side work". **The decode-cap measurement does not settle this, and the record now says so.** The population-level truncation rate is **UNMEASURED** — the stored field is the batch maximum and the frozen runner records it as INVALID for interpretation — so the ordering of corpus versus serving work rests on the response-text fact that **833 of the 1,022 CTI-MCQ rejections were still reasoning and had reached no answer**, and on the field-independent 128-item sample in which **every** unparseable response sat at exactly 512 tokens. On CTI-RCM the serving contract alone may clear the bar. On CTI-MCQ it cannot: the model's own output is too long to finish, and **that is a corpus property before it is a serving property** — the contaminated rows teach the verbosity. So the order is unchanged (corpus first, then serving, then retrain), but the *reason* is now measured rather than assumed, and the one genuinely open lever — how much of the CTI-MCQ gap a larger decode budget would close — is a question that needs a decision before anyone spends a GPU-hour on it [ER §8 question 9].

**All four results must still be reported honestly.** The frozen-surface number is the release number — one surface for every arm, or the comparison is not a comparison [ER §4.5]. The serving configuration is reported *alongside* it, never instead of it.

## 8. Open product questions — the ones that are genuinely open

These are product taste, audience and positioning. They are not measurement questions, and the measurement work does not need them answered to continue.

1. **Which objective is primary — authority or revenue?** They want different first products and different sequencing: authority wants the provable release of §6; revenue wants shape (a) with 8–12 weeks of data collection and no public proof [R7]. This decision precedes everything in §7.
2. **Release licence.** Apache-2.0 matches the direct twin and maximises adoption and citation [R5]. A more restrictive licence could be argued from comparative advantage in defence — but it also reduces the proof-of-work audience, and the twin already occupies the permissive position.
3. **Is the product "a security model" or "a post-training programme"?** Round 8's finding is that senior framing is the whole chain — curate → train → evaluate → decide — and that postings bundle fine-tuning with data curation and evaluation design [R8]. The release should present the programme, with the model as its artefact.
4. **How much of the failure narrative is published under an individual's name rather than the project's?** It is the highest-value single artefact by round 9's reasoning, and it is also the most exposing. Publishing it as the project's record rather than a personal post may cost some of the authority and protect some of the risk.
5. **Which first vertical?** CMMC/DIB (compliance buyer, a calendar-driven deadline) [R6], MSSP channel (margin-driven, integration-heavy) [R6], or individual practitioners and students (the cheapest to reach, the weakest to monetise) [R7 shape (b)].
6. **Is 4B the right point, or should the target be 8B?** The corpus and the capability envelope argue for 4B: the twin is 4B, the corpus's competence is CVE/CWE at 4B scale, and the showcase is clearest against a same-size peer [R5, PR-R1]. Moving to 8B would change the comparator from CyberSecQwen-4B to Cisco Foundation-Sec-8B, a materially harder bar.
7. **Do you want a benchmark contribution, not just a benchmark score?** Round 9's "credible vs dismissed" checklist names a gap nobody in the field fills — limitations and evaluation sections are the least-filled sections of 32,111 model cards [R9]. A *public benchmark for the thing the field cannot measure* — refusal integrity (`CG-09`) or false-refusal on security-specific benign questions — would be a genuinely ownable contribution, and it would be built from public data. **INFERENCE: this is the highest-leverage single decision available, and it is not on the list in any of the ten rounds.**
8. **Rebuild the corpus, or train on the v4 working copy as it stands?** This is the one decision the measurements have now made unavoidable. The v4 corpus carries **all 4,891 thinking-control-token rows and all 23,146 `merged` rows byte-for-byte**, so it would reproduce both the CTI parse failure and the stock-preamble habit [ER §3.3d]. The exclusion is already decided and no authority is outstanding — but executing it removes **20.69 % of the corpus**, including the `merged` stream that carries the CVE/CWE reasoning the product's headline benchmark (CTI-RCM) depends on. **A corpus without `merged` may lose the CTI-RCM gain that §5.3.7 shows is real.** Rebuilding from public structured sources (step 7) resolves the tension at 8–12 weeks of data collection; excluding and re-measuring resolves it in days but risks the competence the product is built on. **That is a product judgement about which competence to keep, not a measurement** — and it should be made before the next training run, not after.
